The Archimedes problem
“Give me a lever long enough, a fulcrum on which to place it, and a place to stand, and I shall move the world.” — Archimedes

That is the frame. AI is the lever. The leverage point is the fulcrum. Governance and operator credibility are the place to stand. An AI programme with only the lever runs out of conviction, capital or political cover before the bet pays back.

Why this matters

The AI business case that fails the board does not just fail. It puts the CEO in the next HBR case study.

The first wave of enterprise AI is now being judged. MIT's 2025 State of AI in Business study found that 95% of generative-AI pilots failed to deliver measurable P&L impact.4 The companies whose bets paid off had no special access to better algorithms. They made better trade-offs at the moment of commitment. Three habits explain most of the disappointments.

All three habits below are forms of the same failure: nobody named the fulcrum. A use-case list is not a thesis. A scorecard is not a strategy. A template is not a leverage point.

Habit 01

Counting the upside, ignoring the bill.

A glossy ROI number wins the room. The change effort, the data work, the regulatory exposure and the reputational risk all arrive later — and quietly cancel the gain.

Upside bias
Habit 02

One number, no reasoning.

NPV answers one question. Boards ask many. Who sponsors it? Is the market ready? What breaks if it fails? Squash all of that into a single score and the only thing a director really wants — the reasoning behind the number — disappears.

Lost reasoning
Habit 03

Scored against a template, not a strategy.

Generic scorecards reward generic projects. The investments that move a company are the ones tied to its specific bet — the customers it wants to keep, the partners it needs, the boundary it has to redraw.

Off-strategy
Where AI is different

AI breaks the investment playbook in four specific ways.

Treating AI like any other capital decision is the most expensive mistake on the table. The economics are different. The risk profile is different. The thing you are actually buying is different. Four differences explain why a generic ROI model misreads the bet.

Difference 01

You are buying a domain, not a tool.

“AI” is to investments what “vehicles” is to logistics. A recommender, a credit-risk model and a coding assistant share a substrate and almost nothing else. They mature at different rates, fail in different ways and demand different complementary assets. Evaluating “an AI initiative” in general is a category error.

Difference 02

The asset compounds. The bill arrives upfront.

Data is the rare asset that gets more valuable as you use it. So do the routines, the talent and the customer trust built around it. The cost — clean data, scarce engineers, change management — lands in year one. The return, if you get the rest right, compounds for a decade. Standard NPV under-prices both ends.

Difference 03

Timing matters more than scale.

Where network effects or accumulation economics are in play — recommenders, data flywheels, fine-tuned domain models — an eighteen-month lead is often unrecoverable. Where they are not, the lead is more fragile. The investment question is rarely “is this worth doing?” — it is “is this worth doing now, before someone else builds the moat?” Without language for that question, the committee funds the wrong horse, or funds the right one past the recoverable window.

Difference 04

The risk is regulated faster than it is understood.

Earlier technologies were judged by their winners. AI is being judged in real time by regulators, journalists and the courts — and the rules are moving faster than the technology. A model that worked yesterday can be unlawful tomorrow. The risk lens has to price that.

Rule of thumb. If your investment thesis would work just as well for a new ERP rollout, you are not yet thinking about AI.

These four differences make the spreadsheet obsolete. They do not make the decision impossible. They make it a different decision — one a generic ROI model cannot represent, and one a fulcrum-led framework can.

Look at every AI bet through the six lenses that decide whether it is a fulcrum or a lever moving nothing.

The six lenses are not a scoring exercise. They are a search procedure. Run an initiative through all six and what comes out the other side is the answer to one question: is this a fulcrum, or is this a lever moving nothing? Score each initiative from zero to five through each lens. The output

What the six lenses are looking for.

Every business model has one to three places where, if AI lands hardest, the enterprise revalues. Freeport-McMoRan found theirs in concentrator yield. Toyota found theirs in supply-chain integration. Neither company deployed AI across the enterprise. They deployed it against the leverage point. GE Predix found a fulcrum in industrial-asset analytics and could not execute against it — which is why the third element of the frame, the place to stand, is in the framework.

The leverage point is the fulcrum. The six lenses are the instruments that locate it.

Run a candidate initiative through the six lenses and one of two things happens. Either the scores converge — strategy fit is high, criticality is high, readiness is real, risk is named and priced — and you have found a fulcrum. Or the scores scatter, and what you have is a lever with nothing underneath it.

Focus is the strategy. One to three named fulcrums beats a list of forty ranked use cases — because capital you spread thin is capital that returns nothing.
Why the lever needs a fulcrum

Five reasons the lever alone will not move it.

AI investments rarely produce average returns. They produce wins that compound and failures that linger. Five asymmetries explain why the same technology, deployed by two different companies, can return a multiple of capital for one and a regulatory fine for the other.

01

Upside is private. Risk is public.

Productivity gains stay inside the P&L. The harms — a biased decision, a leaked dataset, a hallucinated quote — leave the building immediately.

What it forces
Score risk as a negative number, not a footnote.
02

The same tool, opposite outcomes.

A coding assistant makes senior engineers more valuable and junior engineers less so. The headline (“AI replaces engineers”) hides both effects and gets the strategy backwards.

What it forces
Decide which tasks you are automating, not which jobs.
03

Procurement is fast. Adoption is slow.

The licence is signed in a quarter. The behaviour change takes three years. The gap is where the business case quietly dies.

What it forces
Budget the behavioural change at the same line as the licence.
04

Early leads compound. Followers do not catch up.

Data accumulates. Talent clusters. Customer trust hardens. By the time the second-mover has a working model, the first-mover has a moat.

What it forces
Treat timing as a first-class criterion, alongside ROI.
05

Vendors sell capability. Markets buy outcomes.

The capability is real; the outcome depends on the data you do not have, the talent you cannot hire and the process you have not yet redesigned.

What it forces
Score what you will deliver — not what the demo did.

Each asymmetry is a way the lever fails when no fulcrum is named. The framework exists to make sure none of them is left out of the meeting.

The metric

The only AI metric I would put in front of a board.

Capital allocated to economic leverage points, as a percentage of total AI spend.

Below 20%

Sprinkler.

Your AI strategy is a sprinkler. Activity without revaluation. Budget without thesis.

20–60%

In motion.

The default position. A step change moves you up. Drift keeps you here. Without either, you slide back to sprinkler. Re-score quarterly.

Above 60%

Defensibility.

You are building real defensibility. Capital is concentrated where the business model revalues.
